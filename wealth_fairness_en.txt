Analysis of the Limits of Legal Capital Accumulation and  
Disproportions in the Global Distribution of Wealth (1996-
2026)
Introduction to the Paradigm of Economic Enrichment and the Limits of 
Labor Value
In modern economic theory and sociology, there is a fundamental distinction between income obtained 
through the direct application of human capital and wealth generated through ownership of financial and 
physical assets. Over the last thirty years, from 1996 to 2026, the global economy has undergone colossal 
structural changes. This period included the technology boom of the late 1990s, the emergence of the global 
internet, several destructive financial crises, a pandemic, and the unprecedented development of artificial 
intelligence systems. These macroeconomic transformations were accompanied by significant growth in 
global wealth, but at the same time they led to a radical and, in recent history, unprecedented intensification 
of economic inequality.
The purpose of the present study is to conduct a strict empirical, historical, and mathematical analysis of the 
possibility of attaining millionaire or billionaire status under real-world conditions. The analysis is built 
exclusively on confirmed legal metrics: official data on salaries of highly qualified specialists, statistics on 
consumer expenditures required to maintain standards of living, historical stock-market return indicators, 
and documented payments for scientific discoveries and patents on inventions. Any schemes involving 
fraud, corruption, the shadow economy, tax evasion, or the use of illegal financial instruments are 
completely excluded from consideration.
By calculating the theoretical “absolute maximum” financial income that an outstanding individual could 
obtain through exceptional labor, talent, and genius under the most favorable conditions, we compare that 
calculated value with the actual net worth of the richest people on the planet today. This comparison makes 
it possible not only to reveal the scale of dissonance between legal labor-based enrichment and the actual 
accumulation of super-capital, but also to determine precisely the number of people whose financial 
condition contradicts the mathematical limits of legal labor income. On the basis of the resulting conclusions, 
a comprehensive action plan will be formulated at the legal, moral, economic, and social levels, aimed at 
restoring justice and eliminating systemic defects in the global financial architecture.
The Compensation Architecture of Highly Qualified Labor (1996-2026)
In order to calculate the maximum potential for accumulating wealth through legal wages, it is necessary to 
consult the large data sets of the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS), which provides the most complete, 
stratified, and methodologically sound picture of the incomes of highly qualified specialists over the last 30 
years. BLS data are classified using the Standard Occupational Classification (SOC) system and the North 
American Industry Classification System (NAICS), covering hundreds of occupations and industries.
Historical analysis shows that the highest-paid occupations have traditionally and consistently been 
concentrated in the healthcare sector and in senior corporate management. The medical industry dominates 
the very top of the wage scale. This is explained by a complex combination of stable high demand for 
medical services, enormous legal and social responsibility, and an artificially restricted supply of specialists.

An important conceptual aside must be made regarding compensation for chief executive officers (CEOs). 
Although the official median base salary of CEOs is listed at $262,930, total compensation for executives of 
transnational corporations is often measured in tens of millions of dollars. However, this compensation is not 
“wages” in the classical sense. Historically, U.S. tax law, in particular Section 162(m) of the Internal 
Revenue Code, limited the ability of companies to deduct executive salary expenses from taxable income to 
$1 million. This limitation was introduced in order to restrain astronomical salaries, but the corporate sector 
found a workaround.
Before the adoption of the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act in 2017, there was an exception for “performance-based 
compensation.” This led to CEO base salaries being frozen at around one million dollars, while the lion’s 
share of compensation was shifted into the form of stock options (ISOs, NSOs) and restricted stock units 
(RSUs, PSUs). In 2017, this loophole was closed, making the $1 million limitation stricter, but the practice of 
stock-based compensation had become permanently entrenched. Strictly speaking, stock options constitute 
ownership of capital, not pure labor income. Thus, the maximum pure monetary wage paid in cash for direct 
labor, even in the highest offices, rarely exceeds $1-2 million per year.
The Macroeconomics of Survival: Cost of Living and Inflationary Pressure
The process of capital accumulation cannot be analyzed in a vacuum; it is rigidly determined by 
expenditures on basic needs, maintaining work capacity, and meeting the social standards expected of 
highly qualified specialists. To assess these parameters, the Consumer Expenditure Survey (CE), 
conducted by the BLS, is used. It is the only federal U.S. government survey that provides comprehensive 
information on the full range of consumer expenditures.
According to CE data, average total annual household expenditures in the United States in 2024 amounted 
to $78,535, equivalent to $6,545 per month. More than half of this amount goes toward satisfying two 
fundamental needs: housing and transportation. Housing expenditures averaged $26,266 per year (33.4% of 
total expenditures), while transportation expenditures were $13,318 per year (17.0%). Statistics also show 
significant regional disproportions. For example, average annual expenditures in the western states are 
significantly above the national figure, amounting to $91,079, while in the South they are $69,373. Food 
expenditures nationwide average $10,077 per year, of which $6,139 is spent on food at home, including 
basic categories such as cereals and bakery products totaling $804.
However, to assess the maximum potential for capital accumulation, we must account for the influence of 
inflation over the specified thirty-year period (1996-2026). Analysis of historical Consumer Price Index (CPI) 
data demonstrates the persistent erosive effect of inflation on purchasing power.

Analyzing these data, it becomes obvious that for a top-tier specialist, such as a pediatric surgeon earning 
$450,000, expenses required to maintain the corresponding standard of living will be several times higher

structure that, taking into account cumulative inflation of 112.25%, easily reaches $150,000-$200,000 per 
year. This factor is critically important because it is directly subtracted from the amount available for 
investment and capital formation.
The Fiscal Sieve: Taxation of Super-Labor Income
Legal enrichment within the framework of the law requires strict compliance with tax legislation. The fiscal 
policy of developed countries is structured in such a way that direct labor income, meaning wages or salary, 
is taxed at the harshest progressive rates, unlike capital gains, which enjoy significant preferential treatment.
A historical analysis of the top marginal federal income-tax rate in the United States over the last 30 years 
shows its fluctuations depending on political course, but also its consistently high level of withholding for 
persons with the highest incomes:
 From 1993 to 2000, including the technology boom of the late 1990s, the top rate was 39.6%.
 In 2001 and 2002, rates decreased slightly to 39.1% and 38.6%, respectively.
 During the era of tax cuts under the George W. Bush administration (2003-2012), the rate stabilized at 
35.0%.
 With the adoption of the American Taxpayer Relief Act in 2013, the rate returned to 39.6% and remained 
in effect until 2017.
 Beginning in 2018 and continuing through 2026, the top rate was set at 37.0% for taxpayers with the 
highest incomes.
It is important to understand that federal income tax is only part of the fiscal burden. State taxes, which in 
states such as California can reach 13.3%, local taxes, Medicare taxes, and property taxes are added to it. 
As a result, the combined effective tax rate for a highly paid employee-specialist may range from 45% to 
55%. Historical data confirm that the top 1 percent of taxpayers bears a colossal burden: if in 1986 they paid 
25.8% of all income taxes, thirty years later their share had risen to 37.3%, despite the reduction in the 
marginal rate.
Let us apply these parameters to our hypothetical pediatric surgeon earning the absolute maximum for an 
employee: $500,000 per year. After paying federal taxes, state taxes, and social contributions of 
approximately 45%, his net income would be $275,000. Subtracting justified expenses required to maintain 
a high standard of living ($150,000), we obtain free cash flow of $125,000 per year. This is the maximum 
legal surplus that a person can direct toward capital accumulation solely by means of his labor.

Intellectual Capital: Monetization Through Scientific Prizes and Patents
To expand the boundaries of the theoretical maximum legal income, it is necessary to include extraordinary 
events in the model: making an epochal scientific discovery or creating a revolutionary invention that 
changes the technological landscape.
Scientific prizes and grants
The highest global recognition of a scientific, humanitarian, or spiritual achievement is a prize. However, 
even the most prestigious awards in the world have strict financial limits that pale in comparison with 
corporate capital.
 Nobel Prize: Historically, the amount of the prize has fluctuated around 9 million Swedish kronor, which 
is equivalent to approximately $1,100,000.
 Templeton Prize: Established in 1972 by Sir John Templeton, this prize is awarded annually for 
outstanding contributions to affirming life’s spiritual dimension. The distinctive feature of this prize is that 
its founder intentionally set its monetary equivalent so that it would always exceed the amount of the 
Nobel Prize. At present, its amount is £1,100,000, or about $1.5 million.
 Other prestigious awards: the A.M. Turing Award in computer science is $1,000,000; the Kluge Prize in 
the humanities is $1,000,000; and the Crafoord Prize is about $622,000.
Even if one assumes a fantastic scenario in which one person receives several such prizes during a lifetime, 
their combined income would be on the order of $3-5 million before tax. This is a significant sum for an 
ordinary person, but a statistical rounding error in the world of the super-rich.
Patents and licensing royalties
Inventorship has the potential to generate much larger sums. The aggregate private value of patent licensing 
in the United States is estimated at roughly $80 billion annually. Nevertheless, economic history and the 
sociology of science show that corporations possessing legal and production resources almost always 
appropriate the lion’s share of profits from inventions.
Most engineers and researchers working in large companies receive only a one-time corporate bonus of 
about $5,000 for a registered patent, while the corporation monopolizes the rights to use the technology. For 
example, while wireless-technology patents brought Qualcomm around $100 billion, and patents for the 
drugs Lipitor and Humira generated $130 billion for pharmaceutical giants, ordinary engineers, such as Tao 
Luo with 4,602 patents for Qualcomm, receive only their fixed salary.
However, there are rare historical precedents of colossal payments to independent individual inventors who 
managed to defend their rights:
 Lonnie Johnson: an outstanding nuclear engineer, holder of a Ph.D. from Tuskegee University and 
former NASA scientist. He created the legendary Super Soaker water gun, licensed in 1989. In 1996, 
Johnson entered into an agreement with Hasbro that provided for a royalty payment of 2% on “three-
dimensional products.” After the corporation underpaid him for many years, in 2013, after lengthy 
arbitration proceedings, Johnson won $72.9 million in unpaid royalties.
 Chester Carlson: the inventor of xerography, whose patent gave rise to plain-paper copying machines. 
Xerox used his patents to create a monopoly in the 1960s and 1970s. Thanks to a well-structured 
licensing strategy, by the late 1960s Carlson had earned more than $150 million in royalties and in the 
value of shares he owned.
 George de Mestral: the inventor of the hook-and-loop fastener Velcro, who patented the idea after 
observing burrs; it brought him a significant fortune.
These examples show that, under the most exceptional and fortunate circumstances, an individual inventor 
can legally earn from $70 million to $150 million.

Mathematical Modeling of the “Absolute Legal Maximum” (1996-2026)
Possessing precise data on salaries, expenditures, taxes, prizes, and patent royalties, we can construct a 
strict mathematical model of the life of an “Ideal Genius.” This hypothetical person, over the last 30 years, 
from June 1996 to June 2026, combined the highest-paid job in the world, made an epoch-making invention, 
received the highest scientific prize, and impeccably, with perfect discipline, invested all free funds in the 
stock market.
The stock market, particularly the S&P 500 index, is the principal legal instrument for capitalizing savings 
through the mechanism of compound interest. From January 1, 1970, to the end of 2025, the average 
annual total return of the S&P 500, including reinvested dividends, was approximately 11.3% in nominal 
terms. Over the last 30 years (1994-2024), the average annual return was about 9%, which, after adjustment 
for inflation, gives a real return of about 6.3%. The index experienced periods of rapid growth, such as 
31.01% in 1997 and 28.88% in 2019, and deep collapses, such as -43% during the 2008-2009 crisis and 
-19.44% in 2022. In the long term, according to studies, equities outpace inflation, providing a real inflation-
adjusted return of 7.0%. The use of more frequent compounding, monthly or daily, can slightly accelerate 
capital growth; however, for long-term equity investments, annual compounding is normally used. According 
to historical backtests, the total return of the S&P 500 over the last 30 years exceeded 700-900%, 
depending on the exact entry and exit points.
The “Universal Genius” scenario
1. Labor capital: The person works as a surgeon and annually invests his free surplus of $125,000, after all 
taxes and luxury expenses, in the S&P 500 for 30 years. At an average annual nominal rate of 10%, 
compound interest would turn these regular contributions into capital of about $22 million by 2026.
2. Scientific triumph: In 2006, he receives the Templeton Prize. After paying taxes, he retains $1,000,000, 
which he invests in the S&P 500. Over the remaining 20 years, until 2026, at 10% annually, this million 
becomes $6.7 million.
3. Patent jackpot: In 1996, he enters into a contract similar to Lonnie Johnson’s contract and, in 2006 after 
litigation, receives a lump-sum payment of $100 million before tax. After paying 35% in taxes, he invests 
the remaining $65 million in the S&P 500 for 20 years. This capital grows to $437 million.
Summing these fantastically ideal indicators, we obtain final capital of approximately $465 million U.S. 
dollars.
Even if we extrapolate this model to the scenario of Chester Carlson, who could have invested $150 million 
net in 1996 for 30 years at the historical rate of 11.3%, his fortune would have reached approximately $3.7 
billion.
Conclusion from the mathematical model: The absolute mathematical, physical, and economic limit of what 
one person can legally earn over a lifetime, relying exclusively on physical labor, outstanding intelligence, 
extraordinary inventiveness, and the historical realities of the stock market, amounts to between $500 million 
and $3-4 billion under the most incredible convergence of circumstances. Becoming an “everyday 
millionaire,” with a net worth of $1 million to $5 million, is absolutely realistic for a qualified specialist through 
disciplined investment; today there are more than 56 million such people in the world. However, becoming a 
multi-billionaire exclusively through salary, grants, and patent licensing royalties is physically impossible.
Empirical Reality: Statistical Refutation of the Labor Paradigm
The calculations presented in the previous section establish the theoretical ceiling of wealth based on labor 
and innovation. However, these figures enter into the sharpest contradiction with the reality of modern global 
inequality. As of 2025-2026, data from the reports of the largest investment banks (UBS, Credit Suisse) and 
analytical agencies (Forbes, Henley & Partners, Oxfam, World Inequality Lab) demonstrate a picture of 
wealth distribution that completely destroys the logic of linear accumulation of capital proportional to labor.

The demography of global super-wealth
The UBS Global Wealth Report for 2024-2025 shows that global wealth has reached an astronomical $471 
trillion. There are about 60 million millionaires in the world; they make up only about 1.6% of the adult 
population of the planet, yet they control almost 50% (about $226.47 trillion) of all global personal wealth. 
The United States of America is the absolute hegemon, concentrating almost 40% of all the world’s 
millionaires, or 23.8 million people. They are followed by China (6.3 million), France (2.89 million), and 
Japan (2.73 million).
But the true abyss opens when analyzing the category of ultra-high-net-worth individuals (UHNWI):
 There are about 400,000 people in the world with fortunes exceeding $30 million.
 Around 28,000 people possess capital of more than $100 million (centi-millionaires). One third of them 
are based in the United States.
 The highest caste consists of 3,428 billionaires, with fortunes exceeding $1 billion.
In recent years, the wealth of this group has grown at unprecedented rates. Oxfam’s report “Resisting the 
Rule of the Rich” (2025) records that in 2025 alone billionaire wealth increased by $2.5 trillion, or more than 
16%, reaching a historic maximum of $18.3 trillion. This jump occurred three times faster than the average 
over the previous five years. The combined wealth of these 3,428 people is almost equivalent to the total 
wealth of the poorest half of humanity: 4.1 billion people. From 2020 to 2025, billionaire wealth grew by 81%.
According to the World Inequality Report 2026, the share of global wealth belonging to the top 0.001% grew 
from 4% in 1995 to more than 6% by 2025. Within the billionaires’ club, an even more elite stratum has 
formed: 20 “centi-billionaires,” meaning persons with fortunes above $100 billion, whose combined wealth is 
$3.8 trillion, exceeding the combined capital of the 2,082 “poorest” billionaires on the Forbes list.

Mechanisms of Disproportionate Capital Accumulation
If the wealth of billionaires and centi-millionaires, about 31,428 people in the world, is not the result of fraud 
or deception in the criminal sense, then what produces it? The answer lies in the structural defects of the 
global capitalist system, corporate law, and the features of tax legislation.

First, the foundation of these super-fortunes is not wages, but unrealized capital gains. The wealth of 
Zuckerberg, Bezos, or Page is the stock market’s valuation of the shares of their corporations. For example, 
Amazon’s patent for a one-stage “1-Click” purchasing process, obtained in 1999, did not bring Jeff Bezos 
direct royalties as an individual, but it gave the corporation a monopolistic competitive advantage that drove 
up the company’s share price. As a result, Bezos’s personal fortune grew from $7.8 billion in 1999 to $72.8 
billion by the time the patent expired in 2017, and by 2026 it exceeded $250 billion. Similarly, Mark 
Zuckerberg’s patent for dynamic generation of privacy summaries became one building block in the 
construction of the Facebook empire, whose capitalization enriched its founder by hundreds of billions.
Second, a colossal tax arbitrage operates within the global financial architecture. The tax systems of the 
United States and other countries are based on taxation of realized income. Unrealized capital gains, 
meaning growth in the value of shares, are not taxed until the moment the shares are sold. This gave rise to 
the strategy known among the super-rich as “buy-borrow-die.” Instead of selling shares and paying capital-
gains tax, billionaires take giant bank loans at minimal interest, pledging as collateral their shares that 
continue rising in value. With these borrowed funds they finance a luxurious lifestyle without realizing taxable 
income. As a result, while an ordinary doctor or engineer pays about 40% of income to the state, the 
effective tax rate for billionaires can be less than 1-5%. The situation is aggravated by the use of tax-exempt 
municipal bonds, which in the United States, for example, reduce federal revenue by $50 billion annually.
Third, global inequality is maintained by corporate monopolies and the “expropriation” of intellectual 
property. Individual inventors face monstrous barriers. While corporations build patent walls that block 
competition and allow them to dictate monopoly prices, the authors and engineers who generate these 
innovations remain employees.
Finally, the World Inequality Report 2026 draws attention to the fact that rich countries and transnational 
elites extract about 1% of global GDP annually from developing regions, an amount three times larger than 
all international development assistance, through net foreign-income transfers, thereby cementing the 
neocolonial status quo and generating “unmerited” wealth. Half of billionaire fortunes today arise either from 
inheritance or from monopoly position and ties to power.
Blueprint for the Restoration of Justice: A Comprehensive Action Plan
The existence of tens of thousands of people whose financial condition exceeds by orders of magnitude any 
physical possibilities of legal labor-based enrichment threatens the stability of social institutions. Billionaires 
are 4,000 times more likely to hold political office than ordinary citizens, creating a plutocracy that shapes a 
“pro-billionaire” agenda and undermines the foundations of democracy. The increase in billionaire wealth by 
$2.5 trillion in just one year would be enough to eradicate extreme global poverty 26 times over.
To restore justice at the legal, moral, social, and economic levels, the international community must 
implement the following systemic reforms:
1. The economic and legal level: A global tax revolution
The fundamental solution is to shift the burden of taxation from labor income to wealth and capital. The 
central element here is the initiative developed on behalf of the G20 countries by economist Gabriel Zucman 
and presented at the G20 summit in Rio de Janeiro in 2024.
Zucman’s project proposes the introduction of a globally coordinated minimum effective tax of 2% per year 
on the net worth of individuals whose assets exceed $1 billion. Implementation of this measure, applied to 
only about 3,000 billionaires, is capable of generating from $200 billion to $250 billion in additional tax 
revenues annually. Extending this tax to centi-millionaires, meaning persons with wealth starting at $100 
million, would bring another $100 billion to $140 billion. Implementation of the tax can be carried out through 
the UN Framework Convention on International Tax Cooperation, work on which began in 2025. To ensure 
effectiveness, strict exit taxes and a mechanism of a “tax collector of last resort” must be introduced, 
depriving the super-rich of the ability to hide assets in offshore jurisdictions.

At the same time, economists such as Thomas Piketty rightly point out that 2% is only the minimal first step 
needed to win public trust. In the long term, it is necessary to introduce a global progressive wealth-tax scale 
with a maximum rate reaching 90% for ultra-giant fortunes, with the aim of directly limiting the political power

In addition, legislation must close the “buy-borrow-die” loophole by introducing taxation of unrealized capital 
gains. Ideas based on the theoretical Haig-Simons model assume that increases in asset value should be 
recognized as income and taxed in the reporting period, regardless of whether the asset has been sold.
2. The social and corporate level: Reengineering compensation and innovation
At the corporate level, the balance between compensation for management and ordinary employees must 
be revised. The tax codes of states should be changed so that companies in which the ratio of the CEO’s 
pay to the median employee salary exceeds historically adequate indicators, for example 20:1 or 50:1, are 
subjected to severe fiscal penalties. Loopholes that allow limits on the deductibility of compensation 
expenses to be bypassed, such as the remaining loopholes after the reform of Section 162(m), must be 
completely eliminated.
In the sphere of intellectual property, reform is required that guarantees creators of inventions an inalienable 
minimum share, or royalty, from the commercial implementation of patents, even when these inventions are 
created within corporate employment. Patent law should not serve exclusively as an instrument for creating 
corporate monopolies; it should stimulate real innovators and prevent situations in which engineers receive 
$5,000 for a technology that generates billions of dollars in net profit for shareholders.
3. The moral level: Reassessment of wealth and financing the future
At the moral level, society must destigmatize taxation of capital. Super-wealth exceeding one billion dollars 
should be regarded not as a measure of personal success, but as a marker of systemic inefficiency in the 
distribution of resources. According to estimates by the Tax Justice Network, a broader global wealth tax 
could bring in up to $2 trillion per year, which would cover half of the annual financing gap for the UN 
Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), amounting to about $4 trillion. These funds should be directed 
toward the development of healthcare systems, the creation of universal basic social guarantees in 
developing countries, and financing climate transformation, returning accumulated funds to the productive 
economy.
Conclusion
The study of the array of empirical data for the last thirty years irrefutably proves the fundamental thesis: 
achieving billionaire status in the real world is absolutely impossible solely through the application of direct 
human labor, even the highest-paid labor, combined with legal income from patents, scientific prizes, and 
classical market investments. The mathematically verified absolute ceiling for a hypothetical “ideal laborer-
inventor” lies in the range of several hundred million dollars, reaching $3-4 billion in exceptional, 
unprecedented historical scenarios.
The current empirical reality, in which more than 31,000 people possess fortunes ranging from hundreds of 
millions to hundreds of billions of dollars, while 3,428 of them control more than $20 trillion, indicates a 
colossal failure in the economic matrix of capitalism. These super-fortunes were formed not through 
proportionate contribution to society, but through the unregulated rise in the valuation of corporate assets, 
market monopolization, protection of intellectual property in the interests of corporations, and historically 
unprecedented tax loopholes.
To restore economic, social, and moral justice, civilization must transition to a new architecture for 
distributing wealth. The introduction of a globally coordinated minimum tax on super-fortunes, the reform of 
corporate and patent law, the elimination of offshore havens, and the limitation of opportunities to convert 
financial capital into political power are not merely theoretical constructs, but urgent, mathematically 
grounded imperatives. Only by implementing this set of measures will the global community be able to limit
