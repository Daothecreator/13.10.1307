## 2. IRC § 4958 Violations by Tax-Exempt Organizations

### 2.1 Statutory Framework and Penalty Structure

Claim: IRC § 4958 imposes a first-tier excise tax of 25% on the excess benefit received by a disqualified person, a second-tier tax of 200% if the excess benefit is not corrected within the taxable period, and a 10% tax (capped at $20,000 per transaction) on organization managers who knowingly participate in an excess benefit transaction without reasonable cause.[^9]
Source: Fisher Phillips / AHA / Weinberg Lieberman
URL: https://www.fisherphillips.com/en/insights/insights/do-your-executive-compensation-arrangements-expose-your-tax-exempt-organization-to-possible-irs-sanctions
Date: 2026-03-12
Excerpt: "For a disqualified person, the amount of the excise tax is 25% of the excess benefit... if this correction is not timely made, the disqualified person will be required to pay an additional tax equal to 200% of the excess benefit. For organization managers, the amount of the penalty tax is equal to 10% of the excess benefit, up to a maximum of $20,000 with respect to any single excess benefit transaction."
Context: Applies to 501(c)(3), (c)(4), and (c)(29) organizations; safe harbor available via rebuttable presumption of reasonableness.
Confidence: high

---

Claim: Section 4960 (expanded by the "Big Beautiful Bill" for tax years starting 2026) imposes a 21% excise tax on compensation exceeding $1 million and on excess parachute payments paid to any current or former employee of a tax-exempt organization, greatly expanding the prior rule that applied only to the five highest-paid employees.[^10]
Source: Fisher Phillips
URL: https://www.fisherphillips.com/en/insights/insights/do-your-executive-compensation-arrangements-expose-your-tax-exempt-organization-to-possible-irs-sanctions
Date: 2026-03-12
Excerpt: "Starting with the 2026 tax year, Section 4960 excise taxes apply to excess compensation or parachute payments paid to any current or former employee of the organization. Unlike the intermediate sanctions rules, the Section 4960 excise tax is imposed on the organization itself."
Context: Significantly broadens the scope of high-compensation scrutiny for nonprofits; organization-level liability.
Confidence: high

## Cross-Cutting Observations

1. **Enforcement Convergence**: DOJ, FinCEN, and OFAC are increasingly coordinating actions that blend sanctions, AML, export controls, and national security priorities — particularly around fentanyl/cartel finance and crypto platforms.
2. **Nonprofit Scrutiny Surge**: 2025–2026 saw an unprecedented wave of congressional investigations into nonprofit foreign influence, FARA compliance, and tax-exempt status — with explicit IRS revocation requests.
3. **FCPA Recalibration**: The 118-day pause and subsequent Guidelines represent a structural shift from broad foreign bribery enforcement to targeted cases with cartel/TCO/national security nexus.
4. **Intelligence Community Turmoil**: Multiple whistleblower allegations in 2025–2026 (Erdman, Gabbard/NSA, DOGE/NLRB) point to internal conflict over declassification, surveillance, and political oversight.
5. **Wealth Inequality Acceleration**: Oxfam/WIL data shows billionaire wealth concentration is accelerating faster than prior years, with policy recommendations centering on global wealth taxation and an International Panel on Inequality.
6. **Pentagon Spending Opacity**: Despite GAO and IG reports on Ukraine aid drawdown, no new open-source GAO findings specifically quantifying "$21 trillion unsupported adjustments" were identified in 2024–2026; the figure remains a historical reference from 1998–2015.
