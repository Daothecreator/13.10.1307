## Фасет: Финансовые магнаты и хедж-фонды

### Key Findings
- Warren Buffett’s effective federal income tax rate was just ~11–17% on reported income in 2010, while his Berkshire Hathaway shares appreciated by $8 billion that same year; his true tax rate on economic income was estimated at 0.08625% [^1]. Berkshire Hathaway has never paid a dividend under Buffett, avoiding double taxation and enabling compounding [^2]. In 2024, Berkshire paid an estimated $20 billion in taxes on Apple stock sales, making it one of the largest corporate taxpayers that year [^3].
- George Soros’s Open Society Policy Center, a 501(c)(4), quietly donated $140 million to politically charged advocacy groups in 2021, plus $60 million to charitable 501(c)(3)s; Soros personally contributed over $170 million during the 2022 midterms, bringing total political giving since 2020 to roughly half a billion dollars [^4]. Soros has been the target of widespread antisemitic conspiracy theories, including accusations of manipulating world events and funding left-wing movements globally [^5].
- Ken Griffin donated more than $100 million to pro-Republican political action committees in the 2024 presidential cycle and has given ~$59 million to the Senate Leadership Fund and ~$32 million to the Congressional Leadership Fund between 2020–2022 [^6][^7]. Citadel moved its global headquarters from Chicago to Miami in 2022, citing crime and taxes; Griffin has acquired over $1 billion in personal residential real estate [^8].
- Jim Simons’s Renaissance Technologies reached a $7 billion tax settlement with the IRS in 2021 for improperly reducing tax liability from trading profits; Simons personally paid an additional $670 million settlement [^9]. The Medallion Fund generated ~39% annualized returns after fees over 30 years and has been closed to outside investors since 1993, operating primarily for employees [^10].
- Steve Cohen’s SAC Capital pleaded guilty to insider trading in 2013, paying $1.8 billion in fines—the largest insider trading settlement in U.S. history—and Cohen was banned from managing outside capital until 2018 [^11]. Eight SAC employees were convicted of insider trading; Cohen rebranded the firm as Point72 Asset Management [^12].
- Carl Icahn and Icahn Enterprises agreed to pay a $2 million SEC fine in 2024 for failing to disclose that Icahn had pledged up to 82% of IEP’s outstanding securities as collateral for personal margin loans worth billions [^13]. Hindenburg Research had previously alleged IEP inflated asset valuations by over 75% and used new investor funds to pay dividends to old investors [^13].
- Bill Ackman’s Pershing Square shorted ~$1 billion of Herbalife, alleging it was a pyramid scheme; the FTC ultimately settled with Herbalife for $200 million in 2016 and imposed a federal monitor for seven years [^14]. Herbalife commissioned a secret 30-page psychological dossier on Ackman prepared by a forensic psychiatrist, costing $100,000 [^15].
- Paul Singer’s Elliott Management (via NML Capital) fought Argentina for over 15 years after its 2001 default, eventually extracting a $2.4 billion settlement in 2016—nearly 1,270% return on its initial investment of ~$117 million [^16][^17]. Elliott was also chosen as winner of a court-mandated auction for CITGO, tied to Venezuelan PDVSA collateral [^18].
- Robert Mercer, former co-CEO of Renaissance Technologies, combined $45 million in campaign donations ($25 million in 2016 alone) with over $50 million in pretax contributions to ultraconservative nonprofits, plus a $10 million investment in Breitbart and $5 million in Cambridge Analytica [^19]. Cambridge Analytica used Facebook data on ~50–87 million users for psychometric profiling and microtargeting in the 2016 U.S. election and Brexit campaigns [^20].
- The 1MDB scandal involved $6.5 billion in Malaysian sovereign wealth fund bonds; Goldman Sachs received $600 million in fees (9% of proceeds) and was fined $2.9 billion by U.S. authorities [^21]. Ex-Goldman banker Tim Leissner received $73.4 million, Roger Ng $35.1 million, ex-PM Najib Razak $756 million, and alleged mastermind Jho Low $1.42 billion [^21].
- Michael Platt’s BlueCrest Capital Management agreed to pay $101 million in redress to investors for misleading disclosures and conflicts of interest (2011–2015), after moving top traders from external funds to an internal employee-only fund [^22]. Platt, worth ~$18 billion, changed residency from the UK to the UAE in 2025 [^23].
- Ray Dalio’s Bridgewater Associates manages ~$150 billion and opened its first Chinese onshore fund in 2018, growing China assets to $2.93 billion by 2023—the largest foreign hedge fund in China [^24]. Dalio has long cultivated close ties with Chinese regulators and policymakers, dating back to the 1980s [^25].

### Trends & Signals
- **Buy-Borrow-Die Dominance**: The ultra-wealthy systematically avoid income realization. Buffett’s 0.086% true tax rate exemplifies how unrealized appreciation + zero dividends + stepped-up basis at death allows billionaires to pay negligible tax on vast economic gains [^1][^2].
- **Dark Money Escalation**: Soros and Griffin represent opposite poles of ideological megafunding, each steering nine-figure sums through 501(c)(4)s and Super PACs, making individual billionaires among the largest political spending forces in U.S. elections [^4][^6][^7].
- **Quant Funds & Regulatory Blowback**: Renaissance’s $7B IRS settlement and BlueCrest’s $101M FCA redress show that even opaque quantitative and multi-strategy funds face material consequences for tax engineering and conflicts of interest [^9][^22].
- **Sovereign Debt Vulturing as Asset Class**: Elliott’s 1,270% return on Argentine debt and seizure of a Ghanaian naval vessel demonstrate that holdout litigation against distressed nations is a repeatable, high-return strategy enabled by New York and UK governing law [^16][^17].
- **Political Data Weaponization**: Mercer’s funding of Cambridge Analytica linked hedge-fund wealth to psychometric microtargeting and disinformation, blurring lines between quantitative finance, data science, and electoral manipulation [^19][^20].
- **China as Hedge Fund Frontier**: Bridgewater’s $2.93B China AUM and Dalio’s decades-long regulatory courtship signal that access to Chinese onshore markets is becoming a competitive differentiator for global macro funds [^24][^25].

### Controversies & Conflicting Claims
- **Buffett’s Tax Advocacy vs. Personal Strategy**: Buffett publicly supports higher taxes on the rich (the “Buffett Rule”), yet his personal wealth strategy relies on the exact mechanisms—unrealized gains and zero dividends—that minimize his tax burden. Critics note the Buffett Rule would barely affect his true economic income [^1].
- **Soros: Philanthropy or Political Interference?**: Soros’s defenders frame Open Society as promoting democracy and human rights; critics and authoritarian leaders (Orbán, etc.) frame it as foreign interference and shadow governance. Conspiracy theories frequently invoke antisemitic tropes [^4][^5].
- **Cohen: Crime Without Conviction**: Cohen was never criminally charged despite SAC’s guilty plea and $1.8B fine. The “failure to supervise” settlement allowed him to retain his fortune and relaunch as Point72, raising questions about regulatory deterrence [^11][^12].
- **Icahn: Activism or Self-Dealing?**: Icahn’s SEC settlement over undisclosed pledging of IEP shares and Hindenburg’s allegations of inflated valuations and Ponzi-like dividends challenge the narrative of Icahn as a shareholder champion [^13].
- **Ackman: Crusader or Market Manipulator?**: Ackman framed his Herbalife short as protecting minorities from a pyramid scheme; Herbalife and allies (including Carl Icahn) accused him of market manipulation to profit from a short position. The FTC settlement partially vindicated Ackman but did not shut down the company [^14][^15].
- **Elliott: Legal but Predatory**: Vulture funds operate within the letter of the law, yet their strategy of buying distressed sovereign debt at deep discounts and litigating for face value has been condemned by the UN, IMF, and debtor nations as undermining sovereign restructuring and inflicting austerity [^16][^17].
- **Goldman Sachs / 1MDB: Rogue Bankers or Systemic Culture?**: Goldman blamed Leissner and Ng as rogue actors, but the $600M fee structure (9% of bond proceeds) and internal compliance failures suggest systemic incentives enabled the fraud [^21].

### Recommended Deep-Dive Areas
- **Renaissance Technologies Basket Options Tax Shelter**: The $7B settlement involved Deutsche Bank and Barclays selling options that allowed Medallion to treat short-term gains as long-term. A deep dive into the structuring, legal opinions, and why it took years for the IRS to challenge it would illuminate how quant funds engineer tax arbitrage [^9].
- **SAC Capital Insider Trading Network**: Despite $1.8B in fines, the full scope of SAC’s expert-network relationships and the decision not to charge Cohen personally remain opaque. FOIA materials and SDNY case files could reveal more about the “culture of aggressive information gathering” [^11][^12].
- **Mercer Family Political-Ecosystem Mapping**: Tracing the full flow of Mercer money from Renaissance profits through Breitbart, Cambridge Analytica, GAI, Reclaim New York, and Parler would show how a single hedge-fund fortune built a parallel media and data infrastructure [^19][^20].
- **Elliott Management Sovereign Debt Playbook**: A systematic review of Elliott/NML litigation across Argentina, Peru, Congo, and Venezuela (CITGO) would reveal how vulture funds exploit pari passu clauses, champerty loopholes, and New York law to extract super-normal returns from distressed nations [^16][^17][^18].
- **Bridgewater’s China Regulatory Access**: How did Bridgewater secure its private fund manager license in China in 2018, and what is the nature of Dalio’s relationships with Chinese financial regulators? This is relevant to both geopolitical risk and the fund’s competitive moat [^24][^25].
- **Citadel Securities Market Structure Power**: Citadel Securities is the largest retail market maker in the U.S. A deep dive into its order-flow arrangements, payment for order flow, and political lobbying against SEC market-structure reforms would complement Griffin’s campaign-finance profile [^6][^8].
- **1MDB Global Money Laundering Network**: Beyond Goldman, the role of Standard Chartered, Julius Baer, Deutsche Bank, BSI, and Amicorp in moving $7B+ of 1MDB funds through multiple jurisdictions remains underexplored and is the subject of ongoing litigation [^21].
