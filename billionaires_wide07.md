## Фасет: Судебные и регуляторные дела против миллиардеров

### Key Findings
- SEC enforcement against public companies declined sharply in FY 2025 under Chairman Paul Atkins, with total monetary settlements dropping to $808 million (from $1.5 billion in FY 2024), and only ~$18 million resulting from post-inauguration settlements; the Commission is prioritizing individual accountability over corporate penalties [^1][^2].
- Elon Musk settled an SEC lawsuit in May 2026 for a $1.5 million civil penalty— the largest in SEC history for a late-filing violation—related to his 11-day delay in disclosing his initial Twitter stake in 2022; Musk did not admit wrongdoing and was not required to disgorge alleged $150 million in savings [^3]. Musk also faces ongoing regulatory scrutiny over DOGE activities, including potential 18 U.S.C. § 208 conflicts of interest and GAO findings [^4].
- Gautam Adani was indicted by the DOJ and charged by the SEC in November 2024 for an alleged $250–265 million bribery scheme to secure solar energy contracts from Indian state officials, alongside securities fraud and wire fraud charges; the indictment triggered a ~20% collapse in Adani Group shares and scrapped a $600 million US bond offering [^5][^6]. As of August 2025, the Indian government had not delivered summons to Adani under the Hague Service Convention after six months of SEC requests [^7].
- The DOJ Fraud Section’s 2025 Year in Review reported record results: 265 defendants charged, over $16 billion in aggregate intended fraud losses, 25 trials, 15 corporate enforcement actions (including the first corporate FCPA indictment in 15 years), and global resolutions exceeding $1 billion [^8][^9].
- Sam Bankman-Fried (SBF) was sentenced in March 2024 to 25 years in prison, three years of supervised release, and approximately $11 billion in forfeiture for the FTX collapse, though he may serve less under the First Step Act [^10].
- EU antitrust enforcement under the Digital Markets Act (DMA) intensified in 2024–2025: Apple was fined €1.84 billion (March 2024) for restricting music streaming competition and an additional €500 million (April 2025) for anti-steering violations; Meta received a €792.7 million fine (2024) for tying Facebook Marketplace and a further €200 million (2025) for its “pay or consent” model; Google was hit with a record €2.95 billion fine (September 2025) for ad-tech self-preferencing [^11][^12][^13].
- The UK SFO secured a £15 million deferred prosecution agreement with Ultra Electronics in May 2026—its first DPA since 2021 and first corporate bribery resolution since Glencore’s £276 million guilty plea in 2022; the SFO has renewed its focus on bribery and corruption under Director Nick Ephgrave [^14][^15]. Glencore’s individual trials for West African bribery are scheduled for 2027 [^16].
- The World Bank Group’s FY2025 sanctions report showed 4,268 complaints, 65 external investigations, 19 sanctions cases, and 19 settlements; fraud remained the most common allegation (64% of cases), and cross-debarment by other multilateral development banks continues to multiply enforcement impact [^17][^18].
- FinCEN’s Beneficial Ownership Information (BOI) reporting requirements were effectively suspended in March 2025, eviscerating the Corporate Transparency Act’s core transparency mandate [^19]. FinCEN assessed a record $1.3 billion penalty against TD Bank in October 2024 and an $80 million penalty against a global broker-dealer in March 2026 [^20][^21].
- INTERPOL issued a record 15,548 Red Notices in 2024 (+27% YoY), while 2,462 were refused or cancelled for non-compliance (+54%); a new Silver Notice targeting criminal assets was launched in January 2025 [^22][^23]. Indonesian oil tycoon Mohammad Riza Chalid received a Red Notice in February 2026 over the Pertamina corruption case [^24].
- The ICC issued arrest warrants for Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu and former Defense Minister Yoav Gallant in November 2024 for alleged crimes against humanity and war crimes; the Appeals Chamber rejected Israel’s Article 18 challenge in December 2025 [^25][^26].
- Russia vetoed the UN Security Council resolution to renew the North Korea sanctions Panel of Experts mandate in March 2024, breaking 14 years of unanimous support and effectively halting independent monitoring of sanctions evasion [^27][^28].

### Major Players & Sources
- **SEC (U.S.)**: Under Paul Atkins (chair since April 2025), enforcement shifted away from large corporate penalties and “regulation by enforcement” toward traditional fraud, individual accountability, and cross-border cases via the new Cross-Border Task Force [^1][^2].
- **DOJ Fraud Section**: Record 2025 enforcement spanning FCPA, securities fraud, health care fraud ($14.6B in alleged intended losses), and trade fraud; prioritized cases linked to cartels, transnational criminal organizations, and U.S. national security interests [^8][^9].
- **Elon Musk / Tesla / SpaceX / X**: Subject of SEC settlements ($20M in 2018, $1.5M in 2026), ongoing DOGE conflict-of-interest litigation (AFSCME v. SSA, Authors Guild v. NEH), and EU content moderation fines against X (€120M) [^3][^4][^13].
- **Gautam Adani / Adani Group**: DOJ/SEC indictment for bribery and securities fraud; summons delivery stalled by Indian government; group market cap fell by Rs 2 lakh crore post-indictment [^5][^6][^7].
- **European Commission / DMA Enforcement**: Teresa Ribera succeeded Margrethe Vestager; fines against Apple, Meta, Google, and Amazon signal aggressive gatekeeper enforcement, with potential structural remedies on the table [^11][^12][^13].
- **UK SFO**: Director Nick Ephgrave renewed focus on bribery and corruption; new Corporate Guidance (April 2025) and Failure to Prevent Fraud offence (September 2025) expand corporate liability [^14][^15].
- **World Bank Group Integrity Vice Presidency (INT)**: FY2025 data shows steady sanctions, active settlements, and heightened focus on rehabilitation and cross-debarment [^17][^18].
- **FinCEN (U.S. Treasury)**: BOI/CTA regime collapsed in 2025; enforcement shifted toward data-driven operations targeting MSBs along the southwest border and major crypto platforms [^19][^20][^21].
- **FATF**: Continued grey-listing and black-listing of jurisdictions with AML/CFT deficiencies; 2024–2025 report highlights that 69% of assessed jurisdictions show major or structural deficiencies in terrorist financing prosecutions [^22][^23].
- **UN Panels of Experts**: North Korea panel terminated by Russian veto (March 2024); CAR panel extended to August 2026; panels on Libya, Mali, Yemen, and Haiti remain active [^27][^28].
- **INTERPOL**: Record Red Notice volume in 2024; Silver Notice asset-tracing tool launched January 2025; ongoing concerns about politically motivated abuse [^22][^23][^24].
- **ICC**: Arrest warrants for senior Israeli officials; non-compliance findings against Mongolia (Putin arrest failure); corporate crime policy prioritizing environmental destruction and illegal resource exploitation [^25][^26][^29].

### Trends & Signals
- **Shift to individual accountability**: Both the SEC and DOJ are explicitly prioritizing charges against executives and gatekeepers over corporate settlements, reflecting a bipartisan enforcement philosophy [^1][^2][^8].
- **Cross-border coordination**: The SEC’s Cross-Border Task Force (established September 2025), DOJ’s international FCPA coordination, UK SFO’s global settlements, and World Bank cross-debarment all point to multiplying jurisdictional reach [^1][^8][^17].
- **Tech billionaire exposure**: EU DMA fines, U.S. antitrust remedies (Google search default restrictions), and SEC crypto enforcement retreat create a bifurcated regulatory landscape where traditional antitrust is rising while crypto enforcement is declining [^11][^12][^13].
- **Geopolitical obstruction of enforcement**: Russia’s UN veto on the North Korea panel, India’s delay in serving Adani summons, and Hungary/Belgium/Germany’s reluctance to enforce ICC warrants demonstrate how geopolitics can neutralize formal legal mechanisms [^7][^27][^28][^30].
- **Data-driven financial crime enforcement**: FinCEN’s southwest border MSB operation (reviewing 1M+ CTRs and 87K SARs) and DOJ’s use of analytics in health care fraud signal a shift toward algorithmic detection [^9][^21].
- **Rise of asset-tracing tools**: INTERPOL’s Silver Notice (launched January 2025) and FinCEN’s whistleblower program reflect growing emphasis on following the money rather than just prosecuting individuals [^23].

### Controversies & Conflicting Claims
- **SEC enforcement decline**: Critics argue the Atkins SEC is weakening investor protection by abandoning Gensler-era initiatives (crypto, cybersecurity, off-channel communications) and pursuing smaller penalties; defenders claim the shift reduces shareholder harm and focuses on “genuine harm” [^1][^2].
- **Adani indictment and sovereignty**: Indian commentators and the Adani Group have labeled the DOJ/SEC charges as “baseless” and an example of U.S. regulatory overreach extraterritorially; U.S. authorities maintain jurisdiction because Adani Green Energy raised funds from U.S. investors [^5][^6][^7].
- **EU DMA fines vs. U.S. tech interests**: The Trump administration and some U.S. lawmakers have characterized EU tech fines as discriminatory “taxes” against American companies; the EU insists the DMA applies equally to all gatekeepers regardless of nationality [^11][^12].
- **ICC arrest warrants and state non-compliance**: Italy released ICC-wanted Libyan suspect Osama Elmasry in January 2025 despite an arrest warrant, citing procedural issues; Hungary hosted Netanyahu in defiance of the ICC, and Belgium/Germany suggested they would not arrest him—raising questions about the ICC’s enforceability against powerful figures [^25][^26][^30].
- **FinCEN BOI suspension**: The March 2025 suspension of BOI reporting was celebrated by small-business groups but condemned by transparency advocates as a major blow to anti-money laundering efforts and shell-company detection [^19].
- **INTERPOL Red Notice abuse**: Record refusals/cancellations (2,462 in 2024) suggest both increased volume and increased scrutiny, yet the lack of country-specific breakdowns makes it difficult to identify which member states are misusing the system for political purposes [^22][^23].

### Recommended Deep-Dive Areas
- **DOGE/Musk regulatory exposure**: The intersection of Musk’s government role (DOGE), his private companies (SpaceX, Tesla, X, xAI), and 18 U.S.C. § 208 conflict-of-interest provisions warrants continuous monitoring; GAO reports and ongoing litigation (AFSCME, Authors Guild) may produce precedent-setting rulings [^4].
- **Adani Group global financial fallout**: The DOJ/SEC indictment’s impact on Adani’s $2.41 lakh crore debt (29% global capital markets, 26% global banks), potential class-action bondholder suits, and India-U.S. diplomatic friction over summons delivery merit close tracking [^5][^6][^7].
- **EU DMA structural remedies**: The first DMA non-compliance decisions (Apple, Meta) are preliminary; potential structural remedies (e.g., Google ad-tech breakup) could reshape billionaire-controlled tech ecosystems and are likely to face U.S. political pushback [^11][^12][^13].
- **UK SFO individual prosecutions**: The Glencore individual trials (2027) and the SFO’s renewed appetite for dawn raids and corporate bribery cases under Ephgrave may produce the first UK jury trial for a section 7 corporate failure-to-prevent-bribery offence [^14][^15][^16].
- **World Bank cross-debarment cascade**: FY2025 data shows 20 cross-debarments recognized and 26 WBG debarments notified for reciprocal enforcement; tracking which billionaire-linked contractors and joint-venture partners are caught in this net is essential [^17][^18].
- **FinCEN crypto and MSB enforcement**: The data-driven southwest border operation, Huione Group severance, and major broker-dealer penalties signal a post-CTA enforcement paradigm focused on transactional surveillance rather than beneficial ownership transparency [^19][^20][^21].
- **UN sanctions panel vacuum**: The termination of the North Korea Panel of Experts removes independent monitoring of sanctions evasion by Russian and Chinese actors; alternative monitoring mechanisms (e.g., national intelligence, private sector) need evaluation [^27][^28].
- **ICC corporate crime jurisdiction**: The ICC’s 2016 policy paper prioritizing environmental destruction and illegal resource exploitation remains largely untested against corporate executives; monitoring admissibility decisions and complementarity debates is critical [^29].
