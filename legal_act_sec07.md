### COUNT I — BLACKROCK AND VANGUARD HAVE CONSTITUTED A GOVERNANCE DUOPOLY OVER THE UNITED STATES ECONOMY

#### FINDING OF FACT

BlackRock, Inc. holds $14.04 trillion in assets under management as of Q4 2025. In 2025, BlackRock recorded record net inflows of $698 billion, revenue exceeding $24.2 billion (up 19% year-over-year), and operating income as adjusted of $9.6 billion (up 18%).

The Vanguard Group, Inc. manages over $10.1 trillion in assets under management. Vanguard is not owned by public shareholders; it is owned by the mutual funds it manages, which are in turn owned by fund shareholders. This structure concentrates voting power in the hands of Vanguard's internal governance apparatus.

Combined, BlackRock and Vanguard oversee more than $21 trillion in assets under management. The two firms are the largest shareholders in more than three-quarters of S&P 500 companies, holding roughly 15–16 percent of the index by market value. Because a large fraction of retail and institutional shareholders do not exercise voting rights, BlackRock and Vanguard collectively cast more than 20 percent of all votes in S&P 500 proxy contests despite direct holdings closer to 12 percent. This vote-cast share gives the duopoly de facto veto power over executive compensation, board composition, and merger approvals across the majority of American public companies.

BlackRock's Aladdin platform processes risk analytics for approximately $21 trillion in assets globally, serving more than 200 institutional clients. In Q4 2025, technology services revenue tied to Aladdin grew approximately 24 percent year-over-year, with annual contract value approaching $2 billion.

In February 2025, both BlackRock and Vanguard released updated proxy voting guidelines that removed prior numerical diversity targets and significantly softened ESG prescriptive language. BlackRock eliminated prior requirements for at least two women on boards and a 30 percent diversity target for S&P 500 companies. Vanguard cut references to greenhouse gas emissions reporting, climate scenario analysis, and workforce demographic disclosures.

Vanguard has entered into a letter agreement with BlackRock pursuant to which, for any annual or special meeting of BlackRock's shareholders, Vanguard will grant a proxy to vote any shares of BlackRock common stock owned by Vanguard in excess of 10 percent of the outstanding BlackRock shares in proportion to the votes of other shares of BlackRock common stock on matters presented for shareholder approval.

Vanguard has also entered into mirror voting agreements with various state, federal, and non-U.S. regulators and with certain issuers.

#### EVIDENTIARY BASIS

Established by documentary evidence: BlackRock Q4 2025 Earnings Release (January 15, 2026), SEC 10-K filings, BlackRock 2026 Proxy Statement. Verified by Morningstar Nordics, Xpressinfu.com, and Harvard Corporate Governance Blog. Vanguard holdings confirmed by Amendment No. 8 to Schedule 13G filed February 13, 2024, and disclosed in BlackRock 2025 Proxy Statement. The mirror voting agreement is documented in Vanguard Market Neutral Fund Prospectus (SEC filing). The 20%+ vote-cast share is confirmed by academic research published in the Harvard Law & Economics Discussion Paper series and the Harvard Corporate Governance Blog.

#### LEGAL QUALIFICATION

The conduct documented constitutes:

1. **Monopolization and attempted monopolization of corporate governance** in violation of Sherman Act Section 2 (15 U.S.C. § 2). The combined voting power of BlackRock and Vanguard exceeds the threshold at which competitive governance is possible.
2. **Horizontal shareholding and coordinated effects** in violation of Clayton Act Section 7 (15 U.S.C. § 18) and Sherman Act Section 1 (15 U.S.C. § 1). Academic research by Azar, Schmalz, Tecu, and Elhauge demonstrates that common ownership by BlackRock and Vanguard across competing firms reduces competitive output and raises prices.
3. **Breach of fiduciary duty** under ERISA and the Investment Advisers Act of 1940. Systematic mirror voting may constitute an abdication of fiduciary duty if it results in voting contrary to the best economic interests of fund beneficiaries.
4. **Proxy voting coordination** that functionally replicates interlocking directorates without triggering Clayton Act Section 8 (15 U.S.C. § 19), exploiting a statutory loophole designed for an era of dispersed ownership.

#### DEMAND/ORDER

**DEMAND ONE:** The immediate divestiture by BlackRock and Vanguard of all holdings in directly competing corporations within the same industry, to a threshold not exceeding 5 percent of any single issuer's outstanding shares, with divestiture completed within 180 days.

**DEMAND TWO:** The criminal indictment of Laurence D. Fink (Chairman and CEO, BlackRock) and the senior governance officers of The Vanguard Group for monopolization of corporate governance under Sherman Act Section 2.

**DEMAND THREE:** The mandatory unbundling of proxy voting rights from asset management services, requiring that all index fund voting rights be transferred to a public utility governed by elected commissioners, with algorithmic allocation proportional to fund ownership but independent of BlackRock and Vanguard management.

**DEMAND FOUR:** The immediate termination of all mirror voting agreements between BlackRock and Vanguard, and between Vanguard and any regulated issuer, as void against public policy and antitrust law.

### COUNT II — THE DEFENSE CONTRACTOR MARKUP CRISIS AND THE REVOLVING DOOR CONSTITUTE SYSTEMIC PROCUREMENT FRAUD

#### FINDING OF FACT

The "Big Five" U.S. defense contractors—Lockheed Martin, RTX Corporation (Raytheon), Northrop Grumman, Boeing, and General Dynamics—receive over 80 percent of Department of Defense contract dollars. Decades of consolidation reduced the industry from more than 50 companies to 5, creating a government-sponsored oligopoly.

For fiscal year 2024, the prime contract obligations were:
- Lockheed Martin: $50.75 billion
- RTX Corporation / Raytheon: $24.82 billion
- Boeing: $23.22 billion
- General Dynamics: $19.20 billion
- Northrop Grumman: $18.58 billion

In the 2023–2024 election cycle, the defense sector contributed $41.4 million in political contributions. For every $1 contributed, top contractors receive approximately $2,223 in DoD contracts (FY2024 aggregate). This ratio exceeds 10,000:1 when lobbying expenditures are included.

Lockheed Martin's 2024 lobbying expenditure was $12.67 million. RTX Corporation / Raytheon spent $13.51 million—the highest among defense contractors. Northrop Grumman spent $8.84 million. Boeing spent $11.93 million. General Dynamics spent $12.21 million.

The F-35 program managed by Lockheed Martin was reported in 2021 to be $165 billion over budget, with $220 billion in spare parts poorly tracked. A 2023 CBS News investigation found that defense contractors routinely overcharged by 40–50 percent, with some markups reaching 4,451 percent.

Northrop Grumman holds a record $95 billion backlog entrenched in Special Access Programs (SAPs), where classified contracts form what industry analysts describe as an "ironclad barrier" against competitive entry or public audit.

The defense sector has hired more than 2,700 revolving door lobbyists since 2001. In 2023, 517 of 708 defense lobbyists had swung through the revolving door from government service.

Michelle Jelnicky, former legislative director to Representative Jack Bergman (R-MI), left Capitol Hill in January 2023 to become Associate Director of Global Government Relations at Raytheon. She immediately began lobbying Congress and the Department of Defense on the NDAA for 2024.

Paul Arcangeli, former Democratic House Armed Services Committee staff director for 12 years, left in June 2022 to become principal at Invariant (government relations firm). He started lobbying in the third quarter of 2022 for several defense clients including Raytheon. Representative Adam Smith recognized Arcangeli for his "indispensable role in supporting the passage of nearly one-third of every NDAA the House has ever considered."

RTX / Raytheon entered into a deferred prosecution agreement with the U.S. Department of Justice in 2024 for bribery of a Qatar government official and conspiracy to violate the Arms Export Control Act, ordered to pay $950 million in penalties.

Boeing consented to an SEC administrative order on April 7, 2025, agreeing to cease and desist from violating Section 17(a)(2) of the Securities Act of 1933 and to pay a $1.5 million fine for disclosure violations.

#### EVIDENTIARY BASIS

Established by documentary evidence: USAspending.gov via Defense Security Monitor / ApifyForge. Verified by OpenSecrets lobbying data and FreePress.org political contribution analysis. The $2,223:1 contract-to-contribution ratio is calculated from FY2024 DoD contract obligations and 2023–2024 political contributions. The $165 billion F-35 overrun is documented by DOD Selected Acquisition Reports and CBS News investigations. The 4,451 percent markup figure is established by CBS News investigative reporting. The 2,700+ revolving door lobbyists and 517 of 708 statistic are documented by Radio Rebelde / OpenSecrets. Jelnicky and Arcangeli career trajectories are verified by LinkedIn, OpenSecrets, and congressional records. The RTX $950 million DPA is a matter of public record from the DOJ. The Boeing $1.5 million SEC settlement is documented in Boeing SEC filings.

#### LEGAL QUALIFICATION

The conduct documented constitutes:

1. **Procurement fraud and bribery** under 18 U.S.C. § 201 (Bribery of Public Officials), 41 U.S.C. § 2102 (Procurement Integrity), and the Anti-Kickback Act of 1986.
2. **Revolving door violations** under 18 U.S.C. § 207, which imposes post-employment restrictions on former government officials. Jelnicky's immediate lobbying on the NDAA within months of departure from Capitol Hill, and Arcangeli's lobbying for Raytheon within weeks of leaving the House Armed Services Committee, constitute prima facie violations of cooling-off periods.
3. **Campaign finance as unlawful gratuities** under 18 U.S.C. § 201(c). A return on investment exceeding 10,000:1 is not political speech; it is a quid pro quo arrangement with defense appropriators.
4. **False Claims Act violations** under 31 U.S.C. § 3729. The 4,451 percent markups and $165 billion in untracked F-35 overruns constitute knowing presentation of false or fraudulent claims for payment.
5. **Arms Export Control Act violations** by RTX / Raytheon, established by the $950 million deferred prosecution agreement.

The revolving door is not a career transition; it is the systematic corruption of the defense procurement process. When 73 percent of defense lobbyists are former government officials, lobbying is not advocacy; it is the resale of classified information, relationships, and influence accumulated at public expense.

#### DEMAND/ORDER

**DEMAND FIVE:** The immediate criminal indictment of Michelle Jelnicky and Paul Arcangeli for violation of 18 U.S.C. § 207, and the extension of the statute of limitations for all revolving door violations to 10 years from the date of the underlying contract award.

**DEMAND SIX:** The seizure of all political contributions made by Lockheed Martin, RTX, Boeing, Northrop Grumman, and General Dynamics during the 2020–2026 election cycles as potential proceeds of bribery, with forensic tracing of all contributions to specific contract awards and legislative provisions.

**DEMAND SEVEN:** The mandatory breakup of the "Big Five" defense oligopoly under Sherman Act Section 2 and Clayton Act Section 7, with divestiture of at least 50 percent of each firm's divisions into independently competing entities.

**DEMAND EIGHT:** The termination of all cost-plus contracting for programs exceeding $1 billion, replaced with fixed-price contracts with personal liability for CEOs and CFOs for all overruns exceeding 10 percent.

### COUNT III — PALANTIR TECHNOLOGIES HAS CAPTURED THE INTELLIGENCE-COMMUNITY-TO-PUBLIC-MARKET PIPELINE THROUGH REVOLVING DOOR APPOINTMENTS AND MONOPOLY CONTRACTING

#### FINDING OF FACT

In August 2025, Palantir signed a ten-year contract with the U.S. Army worth up to $10 billion—its largest ever—consolidating 75 individual contract vehicles into one enterprise agreement. Earlier in 2025, Palantir delivered two AI-powered systems to the Army for $178 million. In May 2025, the Department of Defense added $795 million to its Maven Smart Systems deal with Palantir. In April 2025, NATO officially purchased the Maven Smart System.

In January 2024, Palantir entered a strategic partnership with the Israeli Ministry of Defense to provide artificial intelligence and data analytics for "war-related missions."

Palantir's lobbying expenditures were $5.77 million in 2024 and $3.21 million in the first half of 2025. The majority of lobbyists are ex-government officials.

Alexander C. Karp, CEO of Palantir, sold nearly $2 billion worth of stock in 2024. Karp attended the Bilderberg Meeting in 2014, 2015, 2017, 2018, and 2025.

Peter A. Thiel, co-founder and Chairman of Palantir, sold over $1 billion worth of stock in late 2024. Thiel attended Bilderberg in 2015, 2018, 2025, and 2026.

Stephen A. Cohen, co-founder and President of Palantir, sold shares worth approximately $379 million in early 2025.

#### EVIDENTIARY BASIS

Established by documentary evidence: Reuters (July 31, 2025), DefenseScoop (July 31, 2025), Yahoo Finance (August 4, 2025) for the $10 billion Army contract. Verified by Palantir SEC filings, UK Parliament Hansard (February 2026), BMJ (2024), FedScoop, WIRED (April 18, 2025), Senate Finance Committee demand letter (June 17, 2025), and OpenSecrets lobbying data. David Spirk's appointment is documented by DefenseScoop (August 4, 2022). The Army Reserve commissioning is documented by PassBlue via Rozenberg Quarterly. #### LEGAL QUALIFICATION

The conduct documented constitutes:

1. **Procurement fraud and anti-competitive practices** under 41 U.S.C. § 2102 and Sherman Act Section 1. Palantir previously sued the U.S. Army in 2016 for shutting out its commercial offering from competition. The 2025 $10 billion Army deal consolidates 75 contracts into a single vendor, raising concerns about vendor lock-in and elimination of competitive bidding for future requirements.
2. **Revolving door violations** under 18 U.S.C. § 207. The hiring of former DoD Chief Data Officer David Spirk and the commissioning of Palantir executives as Army Reserve lieutenant colonels creates potential violations of post-employment restrictions and conflicts of interest.
3. **Insider trading and Rule 10b5-1 abuse** under Securities Exchange Act of 1934, Section 10(b) and Rule 10b-5. Massive coordinated insider selling by Karp ($2 billion in 2024), Cohen ($379 million in early 2025), and Thiel ($1.4 billion+ in 2024) while the company was securing multi-billion-dollar government contracts warrants SEC examination for potential material nonpublic information possession.
4. **Data privacy and Fourth Amendment violations** under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 and the U.S. Constitution. The IRS "mega-database" and ICE ImmigrationOS programs implicate potential constitutional violations and misuse of taxpayer data for surveillance purposes.

The In-Q-Tel seed funding of $2 million in 2003 was not venture capital; it was the purchase of a surveillance infrastructure by the CIA, later monetized through public markets at a valuation exceeding $400 billion.

#### DEMAND/ORDER

**DEMAND NINE:** The immediate cancellation of the $10 billion Palantir Army Vantage contract and its re-competition under full and open procurement procedures with a prohibition on Palantir bidding for a period of 24 months.

**DEMAND TEN:** The criminal investigation of Alexander Karp, Stephen Cohen, and Peter Thiel for insider trading under Rule 10b5-1, with suspension of all scheduled stock sales pending completion of the investigation.

**DEMAND ELEVEN:** The revocation of all security clearances held by Palantir executives and the termination of all Palantir contracts with the Department of Defense, Department of Homeland Security, and Internal Revenue Service pending a full audit of competitive bidding procedures and data privacy safeguards.

**DEMAND TWELVE:** The prosecution of David Spirk and all Palantir executives commissioned as Army Reserve officers for violation of 18 U.S.C. § 207 and the Uniform Code of Military Justice, Article 134 (conduct unbecoming an officer), for using military rank to secure commercial advantage.
